# TSYP14 Challenge Briefs

Spoken-style summaries of each TSYP14 Technical Challenge, for reading aloud during the presentation. (AOTN excluded.)

---

## 1. CAS — Edge AI & IoT for Resilient Smart Infrastructure

This challenge, run by the IEEE CAS and CRFID Tunisia chapters, asks teams to build a smart, connected device that thinks for itself. The rule that defines everything here: the intelligence has to run *on* the device, at the edge — not in the cloud. So teams pick a real-world problem, then build something that senses data, processes it intelligently right there on the hardware, and takes an action in real time. It has to end in a live demo — sensing, deciding, and acting, all in front of the judges. Small teams, only 3 people max, and everything stays anonymous the whole way through, even the final pitch.

---

## 2. CIS — Ground Truth Under Attack: Securing the Earth Observation Chain

Hosted jointly by three IEEE societies — Computational Intelligence, Remote Sensing, and Signal Processing — this challenge is about trust in satellite and sensor data. Earth-observation data feeds decisions in agriculture, insurance, disaster response — but what if that data has been quietly faked or corrupted? Teams pick one segment of the chain — the ground link, the sensors, or the imagery itself — and build a system that can verify the data is real and catch it when it isn't. It's a security challenge at heart: provenance, integrity checks, anomaly detection, and then testing your own system against a simulated attack.

---

## 3. CSXCNXYP — City Brain Convergence

This one is unusual: it splits a smart-city system into three layers, and three separate teams build each layer independently — a city simulator, a decision-making "brain," and a security watchdog — without ever seeing each other's code. Then, at the end, all three get connected live and have to work together as one functioning system, even when someone is actively trying to feed it fake data. The real lesson of this challenge isn't about building something flashy — it's about building to a shared interface you don't control, and proving your part still holds up when it's plugged into strangers' work.

---

## 4. IAS — Engineering Industry for a Dynamic Future

Run by the IEEE Industry Applications Society, this challenge is about solving a real industrial problem — ideally one sourced from an actual factory or industrial process. Teams pick a focus: making industrial systems more resilient, more efficient and sustainable, or more digitalized. Then they follow a structured methodology — map the current process, research what already exists, propose and compare solutions, build a working proof of concept, and finally a minimum viable product. It's less about a flashy demo and more about disciplined engineering method, going from a real "as-is" problem to a validated "to-be" solution.

---

## 5. PESxIES — EnergyMind Tunisia

This challenge, from the Power & Energy and Industrial Electronics societies, is about building the "trust layer" of the electrical grid. As Tunisia's grid fills up with smart meters, renewables, and IoT devices, there are more ways for the data to be wrong — a faulty sensor, a biased reading, or a genuine outage that looks the same on paper. Teams build an AI that can tell the difference, explain its reasoning in plain language instead of being a black box, and know when to act on its own versus when to hand the decision to a human. There's also a forward-looking piece: designing how multiple grid nodes could eventually share what they've learned with each other, without oversharing private data.

---

## 6. RASxAESS — The Living Map: Spatial Memory for Emergency Robots

This challenge, from the Robotics and Aerospace/Electronic Systems societies, is about robots working somewhere GPS and networks don't reach — a collapsed building, a mine, a contaminated zone. The idea is that a first robot explores, finds hazards or victims, and leaves that knowledge behind using small radio beacons before it fails or has to leave — so a second robot can pick up exactly where the first one left off, instead of starting from zero. Teams build both robots, plus the bridge that gets that information out to a command post with a live map. It's fundamentally about giving a disconnected space its own memory.

---

## 7. SIGHT — NEXUS: Next-Generation Emergency eXchange for Universal Sustainability

Organized by IEEE SIGHT, the humanitarian-technology group, this challenge tackles Tunisia's climate and infrastructure risks — floods, wildfires, drought, heatwaves. Teams design a low-cost, low-power smart system that can detect a hazard early and get that information to the people who can actually act on it, whether that's a household, a farmer, or civil protection. It has to work under real Tunisian conditions — spotty power, spotty connectivity — and it must include a genuine hardware or sensing component, not just an app. On top of the engineering, teams also have to build a real business and humanitarian case: who benefits, how it gets funded, and how it connects to the UN Sustainable Development Goals.

---

## 8. VTSxEMBS — CabinGuard-ADI

This challenge, from the Vehicular Technology and Engineering in Medicine and Biology societies, is about catching a medical emergency behind the wheel before it becomes a crash — things like a seizure, a fainting spell, severe low blood sugar, or a stroke. Teams build a pipeline that reads in-cabin sensors, detects the medical event, and then safely takes over the car — alerting, slowing down in a controlled way, and bringing the vehicle to a safe stop. It's not just about detection accuracy — teams also have to handle sensor failures safely, protect sensitive biometric data, and justify their design choices honestly, including where the system's limits are.

---

## 9. WIE — Women and Rural Agriculture Innovation Challenge

Run by IEEE Women in Engineering together with the Entrepreneurship and TEMS/SSIT groups, this challenge focuses on women working in Tunisia's rural and artisanal economy — olive oil harvesting, textile weaving, food processing — who deal with heavy manual labor, unsafe working conditions, and almost no direct access to buyers. Teams pick one track — reducing physical drudgery, fixing fragmented supply chains, or improving occupational safety — and build a solution that's both technically real and business-viable. It's a rare TSYP14 challenge that weighs the business case, market analysis, and human impact just as heavily as the engineering.
