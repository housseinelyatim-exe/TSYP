## 1. Problem Statement (form field: 1–2 sentences)

**Final answer (English only, matches `needs_assessment.pdf`):**
Transformer substations in Tunisian factories are already telling us something is wrong: overheating, degrading oil, loose connections, signals their own sensors pick up. ### Affected groups (answer for the form's short-answer field)
1. **Factory owners / plant managers**: bear the full cost of a preventable, slow-developing fault turning into a fire, facility loss, production stoppage, and liability, when the failure was technically detectable in advance.
2. **Qualified electrical personnel ("habilités")** responsible for the substation: cannot physically monitor it continuously even during work hours, yet carry the responsibility if a developing fault is missed.
3. **Non-qualified factory floor workers**: work near a room they're barred from entering and can't verify; if they notice a suspicious noise or smell, they have no way to check it or confirm anyone is watching at that moment.
4. **Night/weekend skeleton staff (security, guards)**: physically on-site during the exact hours the alarm is most likely to go unanswered, but not electrically qualified and with no channel telling them an alarm is active elsewhere on site.
5. **Civil protection / emergency responders**: their intervention point is set by the failure of the alerting gap, not by the sensors, they're systematically pushed into fighting an already-visible fire instead of acting on an early signal.

**"Biggest thing learned" — form answer (draft, refine if needed):**
Across all four interviews, the clearest theme was that current security measures are already basic, largely installed to meet STEG's minimum requirement rather than to actually monitor risk, and that once a fault starts developing there's no fast way for anyone on-site to intervene. The ICAR technical-director interviews confirmed the transformer failure risk is a genuine, high-risk issue across Tunisian factories generally, not specific to one site, which changed our understanding from "one company's local problem" to a sector-wide gap. Separately, the interview with an industrial-organization/technico-financial expert surfaced an angle we hadn't fully accounted for: even where factories already have an ERP/CMMS system, the maintenance module specifically is too hard to use for floor-level and night staff, so the gap isn't just "no tooling," it's "the tooling that exists doesn't get used."
